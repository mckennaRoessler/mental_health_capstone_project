\section{Introduction}
We've all been impacted by mental health at some point before. Whether through personal experience, 
the experiences of friends or loved ones, or even coworkers and peers, everyone has seen what the human mind is capable of. We all experience the world, after all; just in different ways. As a student of computer science and a psychology degree holder, my goal has long been to find a way to combine my admittedly vastly different areas of interest in a way that helps people. Mental health was and is my passion within the psychology field, and although I decided graduate school for counseling or clinical psychology wasn't the right path for me, I have never lost my desire to help those in need. Luckily, psychology and computer science aren't mutually exclusive after all. Technology and computers were designed to help people. They make the things that humans do easier, and help them with daily tasks. Who's to say they can't take some of the emotional burden off our shoulders too? That's where my interest in mental health software comes into play. As we venture into the world of telehealth, chatbots, and mood trackers, the demand for convenient and accessible mental health technology is growing. While it can't replace traditional therapy, I believe software built on familiar devices we use every day can play a critical role in complementing mental health treatment. It can allow users to feel safe and comfortable, as well as feel in control of their treatment and progress. It can provide easy access to resources at the push of a button. It may even be able to predict harmful behaviors and patterns before a user or therapist can. I would like to play a role in developing these helpful tools so more people can have access to the help and resources they need. This capstone project is my first step into this field. The project takes the form of an Android phone app and allows users to track their mood every day, allowing them to compare their data over longer periods of time and pick up on patterns of symptoms. It also allows them to write out their thoughts more specifically and recommends resources and articles that may be of interest to them based on their symptoms and concerns. This application allows for a daily opportunity for users to check in with themselves, which could help them discover what further aid and resources they need.

News feed: This scrollable box in the middle of the homescreen will be filled with articles and links related to recent research and developments within mental health, psychology, and general healthcare that could be relevant to the user. Clicking on an article will direct them to the article at its source online.

Coping Techniques button: This button will bring the user to a page where they can find a list of coping techniques for a variety of mental health symptoms. They will also be able to enter their own, and as such the page will have a section dedicated to the user's previous responses.

Resources button: This button will open a different right-side navbar that will include a list of resources for the user, such as hotlines for emergency support, crisis text line, warm lines for less urgent support, and national resources specific to the user’s symptoms.

\section{Development Process}

Developing this application has been an excellent learning experience. Rather than simply open a code editor and start building, I was introduced to a more practical and realistic method of project planning that better prepared me for the coding process itself by requiring me to develop a clear picture of my goals ahead of time. This was called "requirements gathering," which had me asking more concrete questions than simply what kind of app I wanted to make. There were questions about the individual pieces; did I want the app to be for desktop computers or mobile phones? What operating system would I want the app to be compatible with? Would data be stored locally or in the cloud? All of these questions were important for deciding how to design the application as well as what tools I would need to do so. I decided I wanted to create an app related to mental health for Android phones - the type of phone that I have and therefore am more familiar with. From there, I had to decide whether to develop the app in Visual Studio or Android Studio - two popular Android development platforms. I researched the differences between the two and decided that Android Studio seemed a lot more streamlined specifically for Android development than Visual Studio. Throughout this process, I also worked on a "specifications document" (otherwise shortened to "spec doc") for my project. This included writing a main purpose and introduction describing my inspiration for the app and what it would be designed to do. I also drew sketches of what different screens within the application would look like, complete with buttons and links directing the user to other pages, and written descriptions of every function on each of these screens. The idea was to be as specific as possible so there was no confusion and I could have a much clearer idea of exactly what each page would look like and the functionality I would build into it when the time came to program the app. Finally, I created a global navigation chart, which listed all of the pages and described both verbally and visually how to navigate to each page. This complemented the sketches nicely because after all this planning, I not only had an exact direction for each page, but a navigation chart to reference to ensure that there WAS a way to navigate to every page! With this early planning complete, I proceeded to put the first page together in Android Studio.

I decided the homescreen was a sensible page to build first. Using the sketches I made in the spec doc as a reference, I began by experimenting with the "Design" window in my project. I discovered this required the use of an XML file, so I opened the "activity\_main.xml" file in the layouts folder of my project. It turns out this is the primary page where the default layout for the app is meant to be built.\cite{DeepLinking} Through plenty of trial and error, I learned how to properly add buttons and navbars (though these are called "appbars" in Android Studio). Learning to position these items on the screen respective to my sketches was where I ran into some challenges. Though Android Studio has a drag and drop feature that allows developers to do as the name suggests and select pre-made elements such as buttons and containers and place them on the screen, I found it difficult to actually move them around to the exact positions I wanted them in. Through some research I discovered the constraint layout.\cite{ConstraintLayout} This layout goes hand-in-hand with the design window and is required to fine-tune the positions and constraints of individual items on the screen. It also allows items to be constrained with respect to each other so that when the screen is rendered, items are held in place rather than anchored to the corner of the screen. I eventually learned that constraint layouts could be used within other layouts, such as the appbar layout. This allows for precise positioning within containers. This allowed me to position buttons within the top appbar. Using a tree of constraint layouts, I was able to get all the components of the homescreen put together and looking a lot like the sketch (see Figure\ref{fig:Homescreen}).

Now that I had my homescreen assembled, it was time to begin implementing the button behaviors so I could navigate to other screens within the app. The most important component of this from the homescreen was building the left side navbar that would open at the press of a menu button at the top left corner of the homescreen. I first built the navbar as a separate layout.xml file, but learned that navbars are actually supposed to be treated as "drawers" within their parent page.\cite{DrawerLayout} Drawers are built as menu.xml files and corresponding links and buttons are coded as items within the menu. This allowed me to build the menu to be linked to the homescreen, and now that I had both layouts designed, the final step was to link them together and implement the button behavior. This would involve a series of steps, which included the process of deep linking \cite{DeepLinking} that would let me link the homescreen with an activity to control button behavior. 

\section{Designing and Implementing the Survey}

My background and interest in the psychology and mental health field were the primary motivation for the purpose of this app. I have worked as a research assistant with a professor who specialized in suicide prevention, and consequently I have learned a lot about psychological research and the types of survey items that are often used to understand mental health and human behavior. Items used in a survey of any official capacity should come from research-supported measures to ensure reliability and accurracy. Furthermore, a survey that is done repeatedly (in our case, daily, ideally) should be as non-intrusive as possible. It should be short and only take a few minutes to complete while still maintaining reliability. While considering surveys to use, I decided on the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale, or K10.\cite{K10} This survey has only 10 questions but still includes items to measure anxiety and depression accurately, similar to more common surveys like the GAD-7 and PHQ-9.

Translating the survey into app form took some trial and error as I worked to find a safe and free platform to store the survey where I could actually collect responses. I decided to use Google Forms, which has a good reputation and is free to use, and conveniently has a feature that translates responses directly to a Google Sheets spreadsheet, which can then be linked to a local SQL database. Actually putting the survey together was only one part of the process. Another was to implement it into the Android application. Because I had put the survey in an external application, I had obviously decided not to hard code the items into the app. Instead, my goal was to embed it into the survey screen as it appeared in Google Forms. While learning how to do this, I discovered the WebView feature in Android Studio.\cite{Webview} This allows developers to embed a webpage into an XML screen by creating a WebView window as follows:

\begin{lstlisting}
<WebView
        android:id="@+id/webview"
        android:layout_width="0dp"
        android:layout_height="0dp"
        app:layout_constraintTop_toTopOf="parent"
        app:layout_constraintBottom_toBottomOf="parent"
        app:layout_constraintStart_toStartOf="parent"
        app:layout_constraintEnd_toEndOf="parent" />
\end{lstlisting}

However, this is only the visual aspect of making the space for the WebView to live. The programming that will allow the survey to be linked externally is done in the Activity.kt file, in the OnCreate method:

\begin{lstlisting}
override fun onCreate(savedInstanceState: Bundle?) {
        super.onCreate(savedInstanceState)
        setContentView(R.layout.survey_screen)

        val myWebView = findViewById<WebView>(R.id.webview)
        myWebView.webViewClient = WebViewClient()

        myWebView.isVerticalScrollBarEnabled = true
        myWebView.isHorizontalScrollBarEnabled = true

        myWebView.setOnTouchListener { v, event ->
            v.parent.requestDisallowInterceptTouchEvent(true)
            false
        }

        //enable javascript as required for Google forms
        val settings = myWebView.settings
        settings.javaScriptEnabled = true
        settings.domStorageEnabled = true

        settings.useWideViewPort = true
        settings.loadWithOverviewMode = true

        settings.layoutAlgorithm = android.webkit.WebSettings.LayoutAlgorithm.TEXT_AUTOSIZING

        settings.builtInZoomControls = true
        settings.displayZoomControls = false

        myWebView.setInitialScale(1)

        myWebView.loadUrl("https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSexAm_bFycILZNOOr8kyCZy2gTHmSwKHEun0BeEvnh3eckJgw/viewform?embedded=true")

    }
\end{lstlisting}

That's a long method just to display a survey! But this is what tells the activity which XML file to display as the activity is created, and then how to display the WebView and enable scrollability. We also need to allow button clicks to fill in the multiple choice bubbles, enable JavaScript to allow internet/web connectivity, and finally actually include the URL for the survey itself. It turns out there is a lot that goes into embedding external web pages!

\section{Future Directions}

As my first independent full-scale project, the mental health application has been an ambitious undertaking, especially within the time constraint of a 15-week semester. As such, there are some features I would like the app to have but that I have not yet had an opportunity to implement. Even after I graduate I would like to continue work on this project so it can grow to become a fully-functioning app that I can deploy to the public. One of the most important features I would like to add next would include the data analysis portion. One of the biggest learning curves for me was figuring out how to collect and store the data this project is centered around. It is only appropriate that I now figure out how to aggregate that data in the database and display it in the graphs within the Data Tracking section of the app. There will be important considerations to keep in mind for this function, such as the question of how to isolate data for a specific respondant so that their data is the only information displayed; by extension, we will need a way to identify responses from the same user within the database so we know which data to select from.

Another feature I would like to implement is the resources and the news feed. These sections would provide access to outside resources and would likely require updates as new research comes out. I thought that in the resources section, I could include a feature that allows the user to locate local resources. Now that I have learned how to embed the survey (an external item) into the application, I feel better prepared to work on adding further external resources as well.
