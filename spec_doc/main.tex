\section{Introduction}
We've all been impacted by mental health at some point before. Whether through personal experience, 
the experiences of friends or loved ones, or even coworkers and peers, everyone has seen what the human mind is capable of. We all experience the world, after all; just in different ways. As a student of computer science and a psychology degree holder, my goal has long been to find a way to combine my admittedly vastly different areas of interest in a way that helps people. Mental health was and is my passion within the psychology field, and although I decided graduate school for counseling or clinical psychology wasn't the right path for me, I have never lost my desire to help those in need. Luckily, psychology and computer science aren't mutually exclusive after all. Technology and computers were designed to help people. They make the things that humans do easier, and help them with daily tasks. Who's to say they can't take some of the emotional burden off our shoulders too? That's where my interest in mental health software comes into play. As we venture into the world of telehealth, chatbots, and mood trackers, the demand for convenient and accessible mental health technology is growing. While it can't replace traditional therapy, I believe software built on familiar devices we use every day can play a critical role in complementing mental health treatment. It can allow users to feel safe and comfortable, as well as feel in control of their treatment and progress. It can provide easy access to resources at the push of a button. It may even be able to predict harmful behaviors and patterns before a user or therapist can. I would like to play a role in developing these helpful tools so more people can have access to the help and resources they need. This capstone project is my first step into this field. The project takes the form of an Android phone app and allows users to track their mood every day, allowing them to compare their data over longer periods of time and pick up on patterns of symptoms. It also allows them to write out their thoughts more specifically and recommends resources and articles that may be of interest to them based on their symptoms and concerns. This application allows for a daily opportunity for users to check in with themselves, which could help them discover what further aid and resources they need.

\section{Development Process}

Developing this application has been an excellent learning experience. Rather than simply open a code editor and start building, I was introduced to a more practical and realistic method of project planning that better prepared me for the coding process itself by requiring me to develop a clear picture of my goals ahead of time. This was called "requirements gathering," which had me asking more concrete questions than simply what kind of app I wanted to make. There were questions about the individual pieces; did I want the app to be for desktop computers or mobile phones? What operating system would I want the app to be compatible with? Would data be stored locally or in the cloud? All of these questions were important for deciding how to design the application as well as what tools I would need to do so. I decided I wanted to create an app related to mental health for Android phones - the type of phone that I have and therefore am more familiar with. From there, I had to decide whether to develop the app in Visual Studio or Android Studio - two popular Android development platforms. I researched the differences between the two and decided that Android Studio seemed a lot more streamlined specifically for Android development than Visual Studio. Throughout this process, I also worked on a "specifications document" (otherwise shortened to "spec doc") for my project. This included writing a main purpose and introduction describing my inspiration for the app and what it would be designed to do. I also drew sketches of what different screens within the application would look like, complete with buttons and links directing the user to other pages, and written descriptions of every function on each of these screens. The idea was to be as specific as possible so there was no confusion and I could have a much clearer idea of exactly what each page would look like and the functionality I would build into it when the time came to program the app. Finally, I created a global navigation chart, which listed all of the pages and described both verbally and visually how to navigate to each page. This complemented the sketches nicely because after all this planning, I not only had an exact direction for each page, but a navigation chart to reference to ensure that there WAS a way to navigate to every page! With this early planning complete, I proceeded to put the first page together in Android Studio.

I decided the homescreen was a sensible page to build first. Using the sketches I made in the spec doc as a reference, I began by experimenting with the "Design" window in my project. I discovered this required the use of an XML file, so I opened the "activity\_main.xml" file in the layouts folder of my project. It turns out this is the primary page where the default layout for the app is meant to be built.\cite{DeepLinking} Through plenty of trial and error, I learned how to properly add buttons and navbars (though these are called "appbars" in Android Studio). Learning to position these items on the screen respective to my sketches was where I ran into some challenges. Though Android Studio has a drag and drop feature that allows developers to do as the name suggests and select pre-made elements such as buttons and containers and place them on the screen, I found it difficult to actually move them around to the exact positions I wanted them in. Through some research I discovered the constraint layout.\cite{ConstraintLayout} This layout goes hand-in-hand with the design window and is required to fine-tune the positions and constraints of individual items on the screen. It also allows items to be constrained with respect to each other so that when the screen is rendered, items are held in place rather than anchored to the corner of the screen. I eventually learned that constraint layouts could be used within other layouts, such as the appbar layout. This allows for precise positioning within containers. This allowed me to position buttons within the top appbar. Using a tree of constraint layouts, I was able to get all the components of the homescreen put together and looking a lot like the sketch (see Figure\ref{fig:Homescreen}).
